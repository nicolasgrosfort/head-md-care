### Diversification of future practices

The desire to be close to nature is increasingly perceptible within the Swiss population — even after death, which is why alternative burials are multiplying. ==Forests are particularly appreciated in this regard.==

A ==funerary forest== is a space arranged in an existing forest for the burial of funeral urns.

==Unlike woodland cemeteries, where bodies are buried in the ground, the ashes of the deceased are scattered at the foot of a tree or buried in a shallow hole in the funerary forest.==

Funerary forests are located in ==natural forests==, which allows the protection of biodiversity and the beauty of the site.

### Humusation — another way to die

The humusation of human bodies, also called natural burial, humification, terramation or ==human composting==, is an alternative funerary practice that consists of placing a deceased human body in an environment rich in organic matter so that it decomposes naturally.

The decomposition process is accelerated by the presence of microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi, which transform body tissues into a material rich in humus called compost.

==The compost produced can be used as an amendment to enrich the soil or scattered in a natural location.==


> [!NOTE] 
> Ecological management of death. Low ecological footprint of burial. But not legal in Switzerland.


### Aquamation

Also called alkaline hydrolysis, aquamation is an ==ecological funerary method that uses water and an alkaline solution (soda or potash) to gently dissolve the organic tissues of a deceased body==. This process takes place in a specially designed apparatus, at a controlled temperature and pressure.

At the end of the cycle, a sterile liquid and dissolved minerals remain, which are discharged into the municipal sewage system, and an inert powder composed mainly of calcium phosphates, similar to traditional cremation ashes. This powder can be kept in an urn or scattered in an appropriate location.

### Promession

"Also called ecological funeral, this method consists of plunging t==he body into liquid nitrogen. Cooled to -196°==, the body becomes brittle. A vibrating table destroys it, transforming the organism into fine particles.

Metallic elements (plates from operations, dental crowns, etc.) are recovered using a magnet.

The residual powder is placed in a biodegradable urn. P==romession offers the advantage, unlike cremation, of not releasing mercury vapours into the atmosphere.=="

Developed in Sweden, promession is not legal in Switzerland.

### Natural incineration

Natural incineration, also known as ==funeral pyre==, is a funerary practice that consists of burning a human body in the open air, generally on a pyre built of wood and other natural materials. ==This practice draws inspiration from ancient funerary traditions and aims at a return to nature.==

### Open-air burial

Open-air burial, also known as ==natural inhumation==, is a funerary practice that consists of ==placing a deceased human body directly in the ground, without a coffin or vault==. This practice aims at a return to nature and to minimise the environmental impact of funerals.

Open-air burial uses no artificial materials, ==allows the body to decompose naturally== and is less costly than traditional burial.

Natural incineration and open-air burial are two alternatives to traditional funerary practices that offer advantages in terms of ecology, respect for the body and cost. However, it is important to take into account the legal, practical and emotional aspects before making a choice.

### Burial depth in Switzerland: bodies are buried too deep!

The Administrative Council of the City of Geneva has decided to implement the following recommendations:

- aerate the subsoil by integrating materials more conducive to the alteration of bodies;
- prohibit the use of pesticides by third parties for the maintenance of graves;
- favour the installation of vertical funerary monuments;
- ==modify burial depth== in the future "natural" district at Châtelaine (1.20m instead of 1.70m depth).

Thanatopraxie care — and therefore all attempts to delay the decomposition of the body — is prohibited (at least at Châtelaine).
