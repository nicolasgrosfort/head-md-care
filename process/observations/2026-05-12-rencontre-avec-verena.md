# Context

- **With:** Verena — SEVE agent, Cimetière des Rois.
- **Note:** Meeting was originally scheduled for 2026-05-12 but did not take place. Finally held on 2026-05-15 at 9h45, in the wooden cabins at the south-east corner of the cemetery (along the Boulevard Saint-Georges buildings).

# Key observations

## Maintenance

- **SEVE does not handle burials** — that is the pompes funèbres and fossoyeurs. Burials at this cemetery: 1–2 per year.
- **Tombs without descendants** (e.g. George Favon): maintained by SEVE — typically people who donated land to the city.
- **Only vivaces (perennials) planted:** no cut flowers, despite public demand. Sustainable approach — they reflower every year without replanting.
- **Prairies (wild zones):** introduced this year — areas left to grow wild with mown paths cut through them. *"It gives a magical quality."* Mown completely in summer when they dry out.
- Seasonal rhythm: Spring → pruning, weeding, mowing / Summer → mowing, watering / Autumn → dead leaves moved to under-woods / Winter → pruning.

## Dying tree: Pain-Mugot

- A rare **Pain-Mugot tree**, present since 1943 or 1946, is dying. Verena is growing climbing plants on its dead branches to decorate them — **avoiding having to uproot the tree**.
- Care through transformation, not elimination.

## Dead leaves and humus

- **Dead leaves no longer raked away** — placed in under-woods to form humus (natural compost, beneficial to soil health).
- Before: the cemetery had to be perfect — no dead leaves, no weeds, no dead branches.
- Now: letting natural cycles run. **20 years without pesticides in Geneva** — wildflowers are returning to prairies.
- Non-action (not raking) as ecological care.

## Untreated tombs

- Some tombs are not maintained because they **host rare mosses or protected organisms** growing on non-endemic stone (calcite, granite). They cannot legally be touched.
- **Hélène Burgisser** — wrote *Des murs vivants*, catalogued the rare mosses at the cemetery. Worth contacting.
- **Karcher paradox:** the more you pressure-wash a tombstone to remove moss, the more porous the stone becomes — and the faster the moss regrows.
- Notable tomb: **René Gouzy** (geographer and explorer) — particularly important for its mosses.
- Families retain the right to clean their own tombs: *"Toutefois, les familles ont le droit de les nettoyer."*
- ¾ of bodies now cremated — the soil decomposition problem is less acute here than at Saint-Georges cemetery.

## Cemetery usage

- Few recueillement visitors. Many tombs classified as heritage monuments to protect them from demolition — typically people who contributed to Geneva through land donations or public service.
- Heavy tourist traffic: pilgrimages, especially to **Borges's tomb**.
- **Georges Favon tomb:** shaped like the Cervin (Matterhorn), possibly with Masonic symbols — described as the most *alive* tomb in the cemetery.
- **Calvin** may not actually be buried here, despite common belief. The cemetery was originally a hospital for plague victims.
- Current uses: recueillement, park, museum, yoga classes, children playing around tombs.
- Quote: *"Si tu creuses dans le sol, tu trouveras des os, partout."*
- Book lent: **Patrice Rossel — "Une Visite du cimetière de Plainpalais"** (anecdotes about the cemetery).

# Key ideas

- **"Laisser faire" / "ne rien faire" as care:** leaving moss on tombs, not raking leaves — non-action is a deliberate gesture of ecological care.
- **Life grows on the dead:** moss, vivaces, climbing plants on dying trees — the cemetery is a site of continuous life.
- **Death is taboo even here:** yet children play, people do yoga, tourists visit. The space is simultaneously a cemetery, a park, and a museum. Death is not the primary experience for its users.
